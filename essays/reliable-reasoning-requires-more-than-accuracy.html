Reliable Reasoning Requires More Than Accuracy

Can language models tell when they are wrong? In our experiments, scored against an LLM judge reference, strong self-discrimination and honest confidence did not come together in any one model.

Based on Can LLMs Reliably Evaluate Themselves? (NeurIPS 2025 Workshop). Claims clarified; see the revision note.

Jae Oh Woo
Revision note

Updated in November 2026. We clarified that the labels in this study come from an LLM judge reference, not from objective ground truth; we separated the per-category diagnostics we measured from the theoretical capacity and sample-complexity result, which is a statement about a hypothesis class under assumptions; and we softened the discrimination/calibration statement to an observation from our experiments. A language model that solves a math problem correctly is useful. A language model that solves a math problem correctly and knows it got it right is far more useful. And a language model that can look at two of its own solutions, pick the better one, and attach a confidence that means what it says is close to the capability we need for autonomous reasoning systems.

This is the question we set out to formalize: to what extent can LLMs evaluate the reliability of their own reasoning? Not just whether they get the right answer, but whether they can distinguish their better outputs from worse ones, and whether their confidence in that choice is calibrated.

Being Right About Yourself and Knowing How Sure to Be Are Different Things

Most evaluation protocols score the final answer, quietly assuming that a model which solves more problems also judges its own work better. We did not test that assumption directly in this paper, but our self-evaluation data already shows that a single summary number hides a lot.

Take two quantities we report for every model: the self-evaluation error (how often the model's choice between its own two solutions disagrees with the judge reference) and the expected calibration error (how far its stated confidence sits from its observed agreement rate). In our experiments they move separately. The base Qwen2.5-7B and OpenThinker2-7B have almost the same self-evaluation error (0.422 and 0.420), yet their calibration errors differ (0.314 and 0.357). JiuZhang3.0-7B has the lowest calibration error of all eleven models (0.209) while its self-evaluation error (0.484) is among the higher ones; s1.1-7B has the lowest self-evaluation error (0.378) with a calibration error of 0.285.

Choosing well and knowing how sure to be behave like two separate skills in our data, and an agent leans on both exactly when nobody is checking its intermediate steps.

Self-Evaluation as a Testable Capability

Here is the experimental setup. Give a model a problem. Let it generate two distinct solutions. Then ask the same model: which solution is better, and how confident are you? Separately, an ensemble of three larger models (Claude 3.7 Sonnet, Amazon Nova Premier and DeepSeek-R1), which see the reference answer, judges which solution is better. We use that verdict as the label. It is a judge reference, not objective ground truth: when we say a model "picked correctly", we mean it agreed with the judges. Self-Evaluation Accuracy (SEA) is how often, within a category, the model picks the solution the judges prefer. Calibration Error is the gap between the model's average reported confidence in that category and its SEA. A model that agrees with the reference 70% of the time but reports 90% confidence is poorly calibrated, even if its agreement rate is decent. Both quantities are defined with respect to the judge reference; if the judges are wrong on a question, so is the label.

We ran this on three benchmarks. Math-360 is a set of 360 original problems we wrote across eight domains (arithmetic, algebra, calculus, geometry, number theory, combinatorics, statistics and linear algebra), built to reduce the chance of contamination from pretraining data. For factual and commonsense reasoning we sampled 240 questions each from TruthfulQA and CommonsenseQA and grouped them into 10 broad categories. We also repeated the full procedure on MATH-500.

Self-Evaluation as a Hypothesis Class

To bring learning theory to bear, we need to say what the "hypothesis class" is. A single pretrained model is not one fixed function: change the prompt and it reasons differently, sample at nonzero temperature and the same prompt yields different outputs. We therefore treat a model as a generator of predictors, the family obtained by combining fixed weights with a space of prompts and decoding configurations. This makes self-evaluation a property of the model plus the way you prompt and sample it, which is how it is actually deployed. It is a modelling choice, and the theory below is about this abstract class, not a measured property of any particular network.

The second modelling choice is what counts as an "input". Estimating capacity question by question is intractable and noisy, so in the experiments the inputs are problem categories (Algebra, Geometry, and so on), with questions inside a category treated as draws from that category's distribution. To count as handled, a category requires success across a range of related problems, not on one lucky question.

From Accuracy to VC Theory

The deeper question is not just "how good is this model at self-evaluation?" but "across how many distinct problem types can it evaluate reliably?" That is a capacity question.

This is where classical learning theory becomes relevant. The Vapnik-Chervonenkis (VC) dimension measures the capacity of a hypothesis class: the largest set of points it can shatter, meaning label in every possible way. But classical VC theory handles binary classifiers, not probabilistic predictors that output confidence scores. LLMs don't just say "correct" or "incorrect"; they say "I'm 85% sure this one is better."

We introduce two extensions for this setting:

PVC: Probabilistic VC Dimension
Theory: extends VC to probabilistic predictors
Definition: on how many inputs can the class realize every labeling, each label correct with probability ≥ γ?
In our experiments: the number of categories whose SEA (against the judge reference) is at least γ
Higher = more categories where the model's choices clear γ
C-PVC: Calibration-Aware PVC
Theory: adds a calibration requirement on top of PVC
Definition: on every input, reported confidence within τ of the actual probability of being correct
In our experiments: categories that also have calibration error at most τ
Higher = more categories that clear γ with honest confidence

Each measure has a theoretical definition over a hypothesis class and an empirical, per-category counterpart; the two should not be read as the same thing

A small example from the paper makes the definition concrete: if PVC at γ = 0.8 equals 3, there is a set of three inputs on which the class can produce any labeling, each label correct with at least 80% probability, and no set of four inputs where it can do so. Empirically, a category counts toward PVC when the model's SEA there is at least γ, and toward C-PVC when, in addition, its calibration error there is at most τ. These empirical counts are per-category diagnostics computed from a finite sample and a judge reference. They cannot exceed the number of categories in the benchmark, and they are not a certified estimate of the model's capacity in the theoretical sense.

The two measures nest in a simple way. Classical VC dimension is PVC with γ = 1; relaxing γ can only increase PVC; and C-PVC can never exceed PVC, shrinking further as the tolerance τ tightens. The difference between them, the PVC to C-PVC gap, is the quantity I care about most: it measures how many categories drop out once you insist that the confidence numbers be honest. Note that the ordering guarantees the gap is never negative, so a positive gap on its own is not evidence of anything beyond the definition.

One design choice deserves emphasis. The calibration condition is checked per input, which in our experiments means per category. Our ECE is a weighted average of the category calibration errors, so a large error in one category can be diluted by small errors elsewhere; C-PVC instead tests each category against τ separately. Both work with category averages, though, so within a category overconfidence on some questions can still offset underconfidence on others. A per-category check is an audit at the resolution of the chosen categories, nothing finer.

Capacity and Sample Complexity in Plain Words

Why a VC-style quantity rather than another score? Because, in theory, capacity tells you how much evidence you need before an observed number can be trusted. Our main theoretical result is a generalization bound that mirrors classical VC theory, with the VC dimension replaced by C-PVC. It is a statement about a class of predictors: it assumes the class has finite C-PVC dimension d and that the m examples are drawn i.i.d. Under those assumptions, the guarantee holds once

Sample complexity (Theorem 1, a result about a hypothesis class)

m ≥ (C / ε²) · ( dγ,τ + log(1/δ) )

Here ε is the accuracy you want, δ the failure probability, and C a universal constant whose value the theorem does not give. With that many i.i.d. samples, the prediction error observed on the sample is within ε of its true value and the calibration error within ε + τ, uniformly over the class, with probability at least 1 − δ. The bound needs the true dimension of the class; our per-category counts do not supply it.

Read it as a budget, in the theory. The 1/ε² factor means that halving your error tolerance quadruples the samples you need, and every extra unit of capacity adds another C/ε² samples on top. A richer class is not free: it needs more data before an observed score separates genuine skill from noise. The calibration constraint works in the other direction, since the calibrated dimension is never larger than the uncalibrated one. Whether the capacity of a real transformer is finite and small enough for this to bite is a separate question, which the theory answers only under restrictive assumptions (see the limitations below).

Since a single (γ, τ) setting can mislead, we integrate each empirical measure over the whole (γ, τ) plane, a volume under surface (VUS), giving one threshold-free number per model. We also plug the empirical counts into the bound, with C = 1, ε = 0.1 and δ = 0.05, and integrate that too (SC-VUS). Because the constant is not known and the counts are diagnostics rather than the true dimension, SC-VUS is a relative index for comparing models under the same protocol, not a guaranteed number of required examples. s1.1-7B has the highest SC-VUS in our study (727.3), which, read this way, suggests that more evaluation data is needed before its scores can be compared with confidence.

What We Observed About Discrimination and Calibration

We tested eleven 7-8B parameter models across the three benchmarks. In the workshop paper we summarized the results as an inverse relationship between expressiveness and calibration. Looking at the numbers again, the more careful statement is an observed tendency in our experiments, not a general law:

In our experiments, the model with the most honest confidence was not among the most discriminative, and no model was best on every measure. That is an observation about eleven models under one protocol and one judge reference, not a theorem that better discrimination must cost calibration.

The table supports something narrower than a clean trade-off. s1.1-7B and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct have the highest PVC-VUS (5.83 and 5.61), and their gaps (1.56 and 1.52) are moderate, not the largest; their calibration error (0.285 each) is better than most models'. s1.1-7B also has the highest C-PVC-VUS (4.27). The largest gaps belong to Ministral-8B-Instruct-2410 (1.88, with the highest calibration error, 0.402) and OpenThinker2-7B (1.87), neither of which leads on PVC-VUS. At the other end, JiuZhang3.0-7B shows the best calibration (lowest ECE, 0.209, and the smallest gap, 0.95) with a lower PVC-VUS (4.79). So the pattern is that the clearly best-calibrated model gave up discrimination, not that every gain in discrimination came with worse calibration.

Why not just report ECE and AUROC and be done? Because each answers a narrower question. A global average can look good while one category is badly off, since the well-calibrated categories pull the average down; a per-category check against τ flags that category directly. And a ranking metric such as AUROC only asks whether confidence orders right and wrong choices correctly, so two predictors with the same ranking can differ sharply in whether their confidence values are believable. A predictor that is nearly certain about everything, including its wrong choices, can rank well and still produce silent failures in an agent loop. Our per-category view targets that case, at the resolution of the categories we chose.

Key Insight

Across the model families we tested (Qwen, Llama, DeepSeek, Mistral and others), training paradigms (pretraining only, SFT, DPO, RL and distillation) and domains (math, factual, commonsense), we did not find a model that was best on both discrimination and calibration. That is what we observed for these eleven 7-8B models; it is not evidence that the tension is architectural or unavoidable, and our comparison of training methods is observational, not causal. In the one base/instruct pair we have, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct scores higher than the base model on both PVC-VUS (5.61 versus 5.39) and C-PVC-VUS (4.09 versus 3.76), and its gap is smaller but still clearly positive. We did not test post-hoc recalibration such as temperature scaling, so whether it would close these gaps remains open.

Domain-Specific Self-Evaluation

The picture is not uniform across domains. Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct scores highest on factual questions (TruthfulQA PVC-VUS 6.13) and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct is strongest on mathematics (Math-360 PVC-VUS 4.84), while s1.1-7B has the highest scores on TruthfulQA and CommonsenseQA (6.22 and 6.52) but sits mid-pack on Math-360 (4.76). This suggests that self-evaluation capability is not a single axis: in our data it is domain-dependent, plausibly shaped by training data, fine-tuning objectives and the structure of the task, though we did not isolate those causes.

Calibration, interestingly, looked more portable than discrimination in our runs, although the evidence rests mainly on one model. JiuZhang3.0-7B keeps the smallest gap on every benchmark (0.75 on Math-360, 1.33 on TruthfulQA, 0.77 on CommonsenseQA), and on MATH-500 it again has the lowest gap (0.63) and the lowest ECE (0.169). The replication is not perfectly clean, which I count in its favour: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, comparatively well calibrated on Math-360, ranks noticeably worse on calibration on MATH-500. MATH-500 is also more widely studied and may overlap with some models' training data, which is one reason we built Math-360 in the first place.

Mathematical reasoning, with its clear logical structure, often allows internal consistency checks; factual and commonsense reasoning, where correctness depends on world knowledge, is a different challenge. The domain-level picture is also less tidy than a single trade-off would suggest. On TruthfulQA the lowest ECE belongs to Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (0.247), one of the most discriminative models there, even though JiuZhang3.0-7B still has the smallest gap. With eleven models and one judge reference, we read the discrimination/calibration pattern as a recurring observation in some settings, not a property of self-evaluation in general.

What I Would Do With This in Practice

The framework is a diagnostic, not a training method and not a certificate, so its value is in the decisions it supports and the questions it prompts.

Choose by the cost of a silent failure. For safety-critical settings, where a confident wrong self-check is unacceptable, give weight to a small PVC to C-PVC gap, even if raw discrimination is lower. For exploratory work, where coverage matters more and a human or a verifier sits downstream, a high-PVC model is reasonable. In both cases, confirm on your own data with your own reference labels.

Read the gap as two different problems. Low PVC means that, against the reference, the model rarely picks the better solution in many categories. A large gap means it often does, but its confidence does not track how often. The first calls for better reasoning; the second calls for calibration work.

Check calibration per category. An average calibration number can hide a category where the model is badly off. If a self-evaluator will be trusted in a domain, measure it in that domain, and remember that a good per-category score on the categories you measured says nothing about categories you did not.

Treat sample-size numbers as relative. The theory says richer classes need more validation data, but the constant is unknown and our counts are not the true dimension. Use SC-VUS to compare models measured under the same protocol, not as a prescription for how many examples you need.

Limitations and Open Questions

Several caveats are worth stating plainly. The labels come from an ensemble of LLM judges, not from human annotation, and the judges themselves disagree: in our correlation study on Math-360, Claude 3.7 Sonnet aligned least with the models' own choices (average correlation with model self-assessments of −0.09, agreement 0.53), Amazon Nova Premier aligned most (0.18 and 0.61), and DeepSeek-R1 sat in between (0.01 and 0.55). Agreement with a judge does not tell us which side is right, and every SEA, calibration error and PVC count in this essay inherits the judges' errors. The ensemble is a hedge against any one judge's bias, not a replacement for gold labels.

The theory has its own gaps. Finite capacity follows from standard results for bounded-weight, Lipschitz networks, but residual connections, softmax attention and normalization layers in real transformers satisfy those conditions only partially, so finite capacity for actual LLMs remains a conjecture. The universal constant in the bound is unknown. And the empirical counts are taken over a chosen category taxonomy, with questions inside a category treated as exchangeable; a different partition of the same questions could give different numbers. That is why I treat the per-category results as an audit of the categories we chose and the theorem as guidance about what would be needed for a guarantee, not as one thing.

Finally, the study covers eleven 7-8B models with self-reported confidence. Whether the patterns we observed hold, weaken or disappear with scale, other confidence sources, gold-labelled data or calibration-targeted training, this paper does not answer.

Why This Matters

For autonomous AI systems, those that must operate without constant human oversight, self-evaluation is not optional. In iterative agent loops the overall reliability of the system depends on the reliability of each local check. A system that can solve problems but cannot assess when it might be wrong will sooner or later validate an incorrect intermediate step with high confidence, and nothing downstream will notice.

The PVC/C-PVC framework adds a capacity-based lens for measuring and comparing this capability across models. In practice its empirical side is a per-category audit against a judge reference; its theoretical side says, under stated assumptions about the hypothesis class, how much data a guarantee would require. In our experiments, the 7-8B models we tested did not combine strong discrimination with honest confidence in a single model. Whether scaling, better training objectives or different architectures change that remains open, and it is one of the most important questions for building AI systems we can actually trust.
