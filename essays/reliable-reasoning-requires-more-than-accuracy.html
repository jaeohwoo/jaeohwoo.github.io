Reliable Reasoning Requires More Than Accuracy

Can language models tell when they are wrong? The answer points to a persistent tension between expressiveness and calibration.

Jae Oh Woo

A language model that solves a math problem correctly is useful. A language model that solves a math problem correctly and knows it got it right is far more useful. And a language model that can look at two of its own solutions and reliably pick the better one, with a confidence that means what it says, is the capability we actually need for autonomous reasoning systems.

This is the question we set out to formalize: to what extent can LLMs evaluate the reliability of their own reasoning? Not just whether they get the right answer, but whether they can distinguish their correct outputs from incorrect ones, and whether their confidence in that distinction is calibrated.

Accuracy Does Not Tell You Whether a Model Knows When It Is Wrong

Most evaluation protocols score the final answer, quietly assuming that a model which solves more problems also judges its own work better. Our data does not support that.

When we compared solution accuracy with self-evaluation accuracy at the category level across all eleven models, the two were only weakly related. On our Math-360 benchmark the Pearson correlation was 0.079; on MATH-500 it was 0.191. The individual cases are more striking than the correlations. On Math-360, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct solved 63.9% of problems against 89.6% for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, yet picked the better of its own two solutions more often (60.6% versus 58.1%). On TruthfulQA, s1.1-7B and Bespoke-Stratos-7B had almost the same solution accuracy (44.2% and 45.2%), but s1.1-7B was far better at judging its own answers (62.1% versus 56.7%).

Generation and self-evaluation behave like two separate skills, and an agent leans on the second one exactly when nobody is checking its intermediate steps.

Self-Evaluation as a Testable Capability

Here is the experimental setup. Give a model a problem. Let it generate two distinct solutions. Then ask the same model: which solution is better, and how confident are you? An external ensemble of three larger models (Claude 3.7 Sonnet, Amazon Nova Premier and DeepSeek-R1) determines which solution is actually better. The self-evaluation protocol: models judge their own reasoning against an external judge ensemble

Self-Evaluation Accuracy (SEA) is how often, within a category, the model picks the solution the judges consider better. Calibration Error is the gap between the model's average reported confidence in that category and its SEA. A model that picks correctly 70% of the time but claims 95% confidence is poorly calibrated, even if its accuracy is decent.

We ran this on three benchmarks. Math-360 is a set of 360 original problems we wrote across eight domains (arithmetic, algebra, calculus, geometry, number theory, combinatorics, statistics and linear algebra), built to avoid contamination from pretraining data. For factual and commonsense reasoning we sampled 240 questions each from TruthfulQA and CommonsenseQA and grouped them into 10 semantic categories. We later repeated the full procedure on MATH-500.

Self-Evaluation as a Hypothesis Class

To bring learning theory to bear, we need to say what the "hypothesis class" is. A single pretrained model is not one fixed function: change the prompt and it reasons differently, sample at nonzero temperature and the same prompt yields different outputs. We therefore treat a model as a generator of predictors, the family obtained by combining fixed weights with a space of prompting strategies and decoding configurations. This makes self-evaluation a property of the model plus the way you prompt and sample it, which is how it is actually deployed.

The second modelling choice is what counts as an "input". Estimating capacity question by question is intractable and noisy, so the inputs are problem categories (Algebra, Geometry, and so on), with questions inside a category assumed i.i.d. To count as handled, a category requires success across a range of related problems, not on one lucky question.

From Accuracy to VC Theory

The deeper question is not just "how good is this model at self-evaluation?" but "what is the capacity of its self-evaluation?" In other words, across how many distinct problem types can it evaluate reliably?

This is where classical learning theory becomes relevant. The Vapnik-Chervonenkis (VC) dimension measures the capacity of a hypothesis class: the largest set of points it can shatter, meaning label in every possible way. But classical VC theory handles binary classifiers, not probabilistic predictors that output confidence scores. LLMs don't just say "correct" or "incorrect"; they say "I'm 85% sure this one is better."

We introduce two extensions that bridge this gap:

PVC: Probabilistic VC Dimension
Extends VC to probabilistic predictors
Measures: on how many inputs (categories) can the model realize every labeling, each label correct with probability ≥ γ?
Captures expressiveness of self-evaluation
Higher = more discriminative power
C-PVC: Calibration-Aware PVC
Adds a calibration requirement on top of PVC
Requires: on every input, reported confidence within τ of the actual probability of being correct
Captures expressiveness + calibration
Higher = discriminative AND well-calibrated

Two complexity metrics for LLM self-evaluation capability

A small example from the paper makes the definition concrete: if PVC at γ = 0.8 equals 3, there is a set of three inputs on which the model can produce any labeling, each label correct with at least 80% probability, and no set of four inputs where it can do so. Empirically, a category counts toward PVC when the model's SEA there is at least γ, and toward C-PVC when, in addition, its calibration error there is at most τ.

The two measures nest in a simple way. Classical VC dimension is PVC with γ = 1; relaxing γ can only increase PVC; and C-PVC can never exceed PVC, shrinking further as the tolerance τ tightens. The difference between them, the PVC to C-PVC gap, is the quantity I care about most: it measures how much apparent self-evaluation capacity disappears once you insist that the confidence numbers be honest.

One design choice deserves emphasis. The calibration condition is point-wise: it must hold on each input, not on average. Population metrics such as Expected Calibration Error (ECE) let overconfidence in one place cancel underconfidence in another. C-PVC does not.

Capacity and Sample Complexity in Plain Words

Why a VC-style quantity rather than another score? Because capacity tells you how much evidence you need before an observed number can be trusted. The key theoretical result is that PVC and C-PVC yield generalization bounds and sample complexity guarantees that mirror classical VC theory, with the VC dimension replaced by PVC or C-PVC. For C-PVC, if the dimension is d, the guarantee holds once the number of samples m satisfies

Sample complexity (Theorem 1)

m ≥ (K / ε²) · ( dγ,τ + log(1/δ) )

Here ε is the accuracy you want, δ the failure probability, and K an unknown universal constant. With that many samples, both the prediction error and the calibration error observed on the sample are within ε of their true values, uniformly over the class, with probability at least 1 − δ.

Read it as a budget. The 1/ε² factor means that halving your error tolerance quadruples the samples you need, and every extra unit of capacity adds another K/ε² samples on top. A model with richer self-evaluation capacity is not free: it needs more validation data before you can separate genuine skill from noise. The calibration constraint works in the other direction, acting as a regularizer, since the calibrated dimension is never larger than the uncalibrated one.

Since a single (γ, τ) setting can mislead, we integrate each measure over the whole (γ, τ) plane, a volume under surface (VUS), giving one threshold-free number per model. Because K is unknown, the sample complexity summary (SC-VUS, computed with K = 1, ε = 0.1, δ = 0.05) is a relative index, not a literal count of required examples. s1.1-7B has the highest SC-VUS in our study (727.3): its larger capacity comes with a larger verification cost.

The Expressiveness-Calibration Tradeoff

We tested eleven 7-8B parameter models across the three benchmarks. The central empirical finding is a systematic inverse relationship:

Models exhibiting enhanced self-evaluation expressiveness consistently demonstrate diminished calibration fidelity. The better a model is at discriminating correct from incorrect, the worse its confidence scores reflect reality.

Models like s1.1-7B and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct achieve the highest PVC-VUS scores (5.83 and 5.61), meaning strong discriminative self-assessment, but they lose the most when calibration is enforced, with PVC to C-PVC gaps of 1.56 and 1.52. Conversely, JiuZhang3.0-7B shows the best calibration (lowest ECE, 0.209, and the smallest gap, 0.95) but lower discriminative power (PVC-VUS 4.79). No model excels at both simultaneously.

The expressiveness-calibration spectrum across eleven 7-8B models

Why not just report ECE and AUROC and be done? Two small examples from the paper show what those metrics miss. Take a model evaluated on two equally weighted categories, perfectly calibrated on one and off by 0.14 on the other. Its ECE is 0.5 × 0 + 0.5 × 0.14 = 0.07, which looks excellent. With a tolerance of τ = 0.1, the second category fails the C-PVC test, and the model's calibrated capacity is halved. Now take two predictors on a pair of inputs, one correct and one incorrect. One assigns confidences 0.6 and 0.4; the other assigns 0.9999 and 0.9998. Both rank the pair correctly, so both have an AUROC of 1.0, but the second is nearly certain about a wrong answer. C-PVC rejects it immediately, and it is exactly the predictor that produces silent failures in an agent loop.

Key Insight

This tradeoff is not an artifact of one architecture or training method. It persists across model families (Qwen, Llama, DeepSeek, Mistral and others), training paradigms (pretraining only, SFT, DPO, RL and distillation) and cognitive domains (math, factual, commonsense). Instruction tuning generally raises both PVC-VUS and C-PVC-VUS relative to the base model, but the gap remains. And we argue it is not something a post-hoc fix removes: temperature scaling applies one global monotone rescaling, which cannot correct a model that is overconfident in one category and underconfident in another.

Domain-Specific Self-Evaluation

The tradeoff is not uniform across domains. Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct peaks on factual questions and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct on mathematics, while s1.1-7B stays strong across all three benchmarks. This suggests that self-evaluation capability is not a single axis: it is domain-dependent, shaped by training data, fine-tuning objectives and the structure of the task.

Calibration, interestingly, looks more portable than discrimination. JiuZhang3.0-7B keeps a small gap on every benchmark (0.75 on Math-360, 1.33 on TruthfulQA, 0.77 on CommonsenseQA), and on MATH-500 it again has the lowest gap (0.63) and the lowest ECE (0.169). The replication is not perfectly clean, which I count in its favour: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, comparatively well calibrated on Math-360, drops noticeably in calibration on MATH-500. MATH-500 is also more widely studied and may overlap with some models' training data, which is one reason we built Math-360 in the first place.

Mathematical reasoning, with its clear logical structure, often allows internal consistency checks; factual and commonsense reasoning, where correctness depends on world knowledge, is a different challenge. Still, the inverse relationship between expressiveness and calibration shows up in all three domains.

What I Would Do With This in Practice

The framework is a diagnostic, not a training method, so its value is in the decisions it supports.

Choose by the cost of a silent failure. For safety-critical settings, where a confident wrong self-check is unacceptable, prefer the model with the smallest PVC to C-PVC gap, even if its raw discrimination is lower. For exploratory work, where coverage matters more and a human or a verifier sits downstream, a high-PVC model is reasonable.

Read the gap as two different problems. Low PVC means the model cannot tell good solutions from bad ones. A large gap means it can, but its confidence does not track that ability. The first calls for better reasoning; the second calls for calibration work.

Check calibration per category. An average calibration number can hide a domain where the model is badly off, as the 0.07 ECE example shows. If a self-evaluator will be trusted in a domain, measure it in that domain.

Budget validation data in proportion to capacity. More expressive self-evaluators need more examples before their measured reliability means anything. Since the constant is unknown, use SC-VUS to rank models by verification cost, not as a prescription.

Limitations and Open Questions

Several caveats are worth stating plainly. Ground truth comes from an ensemble of LLM judges, not from human annotation, and the judges themselves disagree: in our correlation study on Math-360, Claude 3.7 Sonnet was the strictest (average correlation with model self-assessments of −0.09, agreement 0.53), while Amazon Nova Premier aligned most with the models (0.18 and 0.61). The ensemble is a hedge against any one judge's bias, not a replacement for gold labels.

The theory has its own gaps. Finite capacity follows from standard results for bounded-weight, Lipschitz networks, but residual connections, softmax attention and normalization layers in real transformers satisfy those conditions only partially. The universal constant in the bound is unknown. And capacity is measured over a chosen category taxonomy, with an i.i.d. assumption inside each category; a different partition of the same questions could give different numbers.

Finally, the study covers eleven 7-8B models with self-reported confidence. Whether the tradeoff weakens with scale, other confidence sources or calibration-targeted training, this paper does not answer.

Why This Matters

For autonomous AI systems, those that must operate without constant human oversight, self-evaluation is not optional. In iterative agent loops the overall reliability of the system is bounded by the reliability of each local check. A system that can solve problems but cannot assess when it might be wrong will sooner or later validate an incorrect intermediate step with high confidence, and nothing downstream will notice.

The PVC/C-PVC framework adds a capacity-based lens for measuring and comparing this capability across models, with guarantees about how far a measured score can be trusted. The expressiveness-calibration tension it exposes poses a concrete challenge: we need models that are both discriminative and well-calibrated in their self-assessment. Current 7-8B models have not solved this. Whether scaling, better training objectives or different architectures can ease the tension remains open, and it is one of the most important questions for building AI systems we can actually trust.
