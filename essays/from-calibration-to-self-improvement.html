From Calibration to Self-Improvement: How One Question Shaped My Research

I want AI systems that can evaluate their own judgments, ask for help when they need it, and improve from the help they get. This is an account of where that goal came from and how my reading of it has changed.

Jae Oh Woo

This line of work started from a hopeful intuition: if a model can pick the better of its own candidates, could it improve itself? A model that can tell a better answer from a worse one already holds something like a learning signal. Its preferences could decide which outputs to keep, which to train on and which to discard, without a person labelling every example.

That intuition only works if the selection can be trusted, so it leads straight to a second question, the one most of my recent papers come back to: when should a model's own judgment be trusted? Trying to answer it split the work into three parts. Can the model evaluate its own outputs reliably? When it cannot, can it ask for help or defer instead of acting on a weak judgment? And can it improve from the help it receives? Self-improvement is not the last item on that list so much as the reason for the list: the first two parts are checks on whether a model's judgment is good enough to learn from.

Over time one question came to sit under all three: what decision does this score support, and at what cost? The other essays on this site each take one piece of the work in detail. This one is about the thread between them: where the intuition came from, which questions it split into, and how my interpretation of the results has changed along the way.

Starting from Entropy

My training is in mathematics. I studied mathematics at KAIST, then applied mathematics at Yale, where my dissertation was on information-theoretic inequalities, limit theorems, and universal compression over unknown alphabets. I was later a Simons Postdoctoral Fellow at UT Austin, and entropy stayed at the center of my work: entropy inequalities for sums in discrete groups, and the entropy and mutual information of point processes. These are statements about how much is unknown, and how much a given observation would tell you.

At Samsung SDS Research America, the same quantities turned out to be practical tools. In active learning, a model chooses which unlabeled examples a person should label next. The whole problem is a model estimating what it does not know. Two of my single-author papers come from this line of work. Analytic Mutual Information in Bayesian Neural Networks (ISIT 2022) derives a closed-form expression for the mutual information between model parameters and the predictive output, using point process entropy, and applies it to Dirichlet parameter estimation and active learning. Balanced Entropy Learning (ICLR 2023) proposes an acquisition function built from Beta approximations of the marginals, a ratio of an augmented entropy to a marginalized joint entropy, which tends to select diverse points near the decision boundary.

Knowing what you do not know is not a philosophical luxury. In active learning it is part of what makes data-efficient learning possible: a model that can locate its own uncertainty can ask for the labels most likely to matter.

That lesson runs through much of what follows. In active learning the model's uncertainty is used to decide what to ask for. Much of my later work asks the same thing of language models, where the "label" might be a human answer, a teacher's correction, or a decision to escalate.

From Uncertainty to Risk Decisions

Another part of my industry work was credit portfolio risk management at Moody's Analytics. That work is not only estimating probabilities. It is deciding how much risk to accept, and what to do, on the basis of those estimates. The aim is not to eliminate uncertainty but to make decisions while keeping risk under control.

A credit portfolio also teaches that individual predictions are not the whole story. What matters is the loss distribution of the portfolio, including losses that arrive together because the underlying risks are correlated. AI systems have a similar shape. The confidence attached to one answer matters, but so does when the system fails, whether its failures come together, and how those failures are caught and managed.

I do not want to overstate the link. Credit risk management and conformal prediction rest on different assumptions and do not give the same mathematical guarantees. What carried over was a practice: define the risk explicitly, state the assumptions and the tolerance level, and tie both to a decision. That practice is why I later came to see calibration and conformal methods as tools for decisions, not only as measures of prediction quality. Read as themes rather than as a timeline, the path runs from mathematics, through financial risk management and information acquisition, to judgment and improvement in AI systems.

Three Questions for Language Models

Large language models changed the form of the problem. They do not just output a probability; they produce judgments in language: a stated confidence, a preference between two of their own solutions, a verdict on whether a step of reasoning holds. Those judgments are cheap to obtain and easy to over-trust. If they are to serve as a learning signal, each one needs checking first, and over time the work organised itself around three questions.

Three questions that organise the later work, read as steps toward self-improvement. In this framing each builds on the one before it.

Question 1: Can a model evaluate its own judgments?

In Can LLMs Reliably Evaluate Themselves? A Probabilistic VC Framework (NeurIPS 2025 Workshop), we treated self-evaluation as a capability with a capacity, extending VC dimension to probabilistic predictors with and without a calibration requirement. In our experiments with 7 to 8B models, scored against an LLM judge reference, no model was best both at picking its better solutions and at keeping its confidence honest. That is an observation about those models, not a theorem; the Reliable Reasoning essay explains what it does and does not show. What the data did show, once we looked further in the follow-up manuscript described below, bears directly on the self-improvement intuition: being good at solving problems said little about being good at choosing between one's own solutions. Across eleven 7 to 8B models, the category-level Pearson correlation between solution accuracy and self-evaluation accuracy was 0.079 on Math-360 and no higher than 0.191 on the other three benchmarks. A model that produces strong candidates is not, on this evidence, automatically a reliable judge of them.

A second line asks whether a model's self-judgments are stable. Structural Uncertainty from Self-Preference in Black-Box Language Models (EMNLP 2026; an earlier version received the Best Paper award at the ICLR 2026 Workshop on Logical Reasoning of LLMs) measures how consistently a model ranks its own sampled candidates. The signal complements answer dispersion rather than replacing it, and how useful it is depends on the task. The lift is largest where answer agreement is least informative, for weaker models on hard contest problems (for Qwen 3 32B on AIME-25, adding it to self-consistency raises the selective-prediction score, Sel-AUC, by 12.0% over self-consistency alone), and the signal collapses on retrieval-style questions such as HotpotQA. The Structural Uncertainty essay goes through where it helps and where it flattens out.

Earlier, Improving Instruction Following in Language Models through Proxy-Based Uncertainty Estimation (ICML 2024) approached evaluation from the reward side. We built an uncertainty-aware reward model that, using Bayesian approximation, scores a pair of responses and also estimates how uncertain that score is. Here the uncertainty is not used at inference time; it is used during training, to refine which data are selected and to guide policy optimization, which improved instruction following on Vicuna and MT-bench.

Question 2: When should it ask for help or defer?

Evaluation alone does not change what a system does. The second question is what to do with an uncertain judgment. In From Debate to Decision: Conformal Social Choice for Safe Multi-Agent Deliberation (EMNLP 2026), we put a split conformal layer after multi-agent debate: the system acts when the calibrated prediction set has one answer and escalates to a person when it has several, with a marginal coverage guarantee at each pre-specified round, provided test questions are exchangeable with the calibration questions. The paper is explicit that the accuracy gain on early decisions comes from which cases the system declines to act on, not from better reasoning. What that selection buys is concrete: on MMLU-Pro at α = 0.05, 81.9% of the cases where the agents ended in unanimous but wrong agreement had non-singleton sets at the final round, so they would have gone to a person instead of being acted on. The paper extends a workshop version at ICML 2026 AgenticUQ, and a budgeted follow-up appeared at ICML 2026 FAGEN. The Consensus essay explains the guarantee and its limits.

Deferral at the end of a decision is one case. Asking in the middle of a task is another. In Knowing When to Ask: Self-Gated Clarification for Hierarchical Language Agents (ACM CAIS 2026 Workshop on AI Agents for Discovery in the Wild), clarification becomes an action the agent rates on the same scale as its other moves. The study separates where an agent asks from what answer comes back, and treats its accuracy gains under a controlled answer source as an upper bound. The sharper finding is that the two can be separated: with automated answers in place of the controlled source, 10-digit accuracy falls by 18.8 points, yet the split between mandatory and opportunistic questions and the ranking of question effectiveness across thresholds are preserved. Where to ask is a problem that can be studied apart from what comes back. The Knowing When to Ask essay covers it.

Question 3: How can it improve from that feedback?

The third question closes the loop. In Reflect, Rewrite, Repeat: How Simple Arithmetic Enables Advanced Reasoning in Small Language Models (EACL 2026 Findings), a stronger teacher model grades and rewrites a small model's answers, and the small model is trained on those corrections through iterative DPO followed by SFT, with a learning-verification step that checks whether the trained model now solves the problem itself. With 700 basic arithmetic training samples, the small models improved on several reasoning benchmarks, though not on every one: for Qwen 2.5 7B, GSM8K rose from 83.1% to 88.6% with a DeepSeek-R1 teacher.

It is worth being precise about what this shows. The improvement comes from teacher feedback, not from the small model reflecting on its own answers. We chose a teacher because prior work had found that self-reflection in RL-trained pipelines can be superficial, and because, as the paper notes with reference to our self-evaluation work, current LLM self-evaluation is limited. So R3 is evidence that a model can learn efficiently from good external feedback, not that it can improve itself unaided. What it does show is that the structure of the feedback matters. In the ablation on Qwen 2.5 7B with an Amazon Nova Premier teacher, removing the structured reflection step lowers accuracy on LastLetterConcat from 53.3% to 35.3%. The ICML 2024 reward model fits the same question from another angle: there, an uncertainty estimate helps decide which training signal is worth trusting.

How My Interpretation Changed

When we wrote the probabilistic VC paper, I read per-category self-evaluation scores as a measure of capacity: how many categories a model can self-evaluate reliably, and how much data you need before that measurement can be trusted. I still think that framing was a useful starting point. But working with the same scores over the following year changed how I read them, and much of that change is now written down in a follow-up manuscript, Calibration-Constrained Diagnostics for Evaluating LLM Self-Evaluation (Jae Oh Woo et al., arXiv preprint, forthcoming). It is an extended and substantially revised version of the NeurIPS 2025 LAW workshop paper, and it differs from that paper in three ways.

What we added. The evaluation grows from eleven 7 to 8B models to 13 models, adding Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct and Claude 3.5 Haiku, across four benchmarks (Math-360, TruthfulQA, CommonsenseQA and MATH-500). It adds robustness checks under alternative categorizations, synthetic perturbation of the judge labels and a re-analysis restricted to unanimous judges; a direct comparison with ECE and Brier score; and a released evaluation artifact with the protocol, prompts, reference labels, model outputs and reproduction scripts (code and data).

What we corrected. PVC and C-PVC are no longer presented as capacity estimates. They are VC- and fat-shattering-motivated empirical evaluation proxies, not literal estimates of VC dimension. The artifact is designed for evaluation, not safety certification. And the labels are judge-reference labels, a majority vote of three LLM judges, so what we measure is agreement with that reference rather than absolute correctness.

What the metrics now claim to measure. PVC and C-PVC are category-level diagnostics over an accuracy-by-calibration surface: PVC counts categories whose self-evaluation accuracy clears a threshold, and C-PVC also requires the category's calibration error to stay below a tolerance. The strictest summary is restricted to the positive-margin region, where high self-selection accuracy and tight calibration have to hold together. The question is narrow on purpose: can this model's reported confidence support a confidence-gated act-or-defer decision, category by category?

With that in hand, my reading of the scores changed in three ways.

From certificate to audit. A per-category calibration score tells you where a model's self-evaluation fails. It is a diagnostic of the model under a given prompt and confidence protocol, not a certificate that the model will behave well on new inputs. What an audit offers instead is resolution that an aggregate number lacks. In the follow-up, JiuZhang3.0-7B is the best of the compared models by ECE (0.199) and Brier score (0.049), yet its positive-margin C-PVC score is only 0.019, while Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct, with worse ECE (0.260), scores about 11 times higher (0.209). The mechanism is cancellation: about 44% of JiuZhang's predictions sit near certainty while disagreeing with the reference 41.7% of the time, and averaging them with its many near-chance predictions makes the model look well calibrated.

From ground truth to reference. The labels in this kind of study come from LLM judges, and judges disagree with each other: in the follow-up, all three judges agree on only 53 to 75% of items. I now treat judge labels as a reference point that should be reported and questioned, not as ground truth. Treating them that way turned into tests we could run. Flipping 10 to 30% of the labels at random leaves the near-zero positive-margin scores near zero (JiuZhang stays between 0.019 and 0.023 at 30%), restricting to unanimous items preserves the ranking structure, and under 7 alternative categorizations the rankings keep a Spearman correlation between 0.75 and 0.92.

From auditing to acting. Finding the categories where a model fails is one task. Acting on them, for example by setting a different trust threshold for each category, is another: it means choosing a decision rule from data. The follow-up manuscript deliberately stops before that step. It is built to diagnose failures that matter for confidence-gated decisions, not to certify that any particular gate is safe, and what it shows is why such a gate needs more than an aggregate score: choosing by ECE or Brier would favor the model that rarely reaches the positive-margin region. My current view, which I hold as a working hypothesis rather than a published result, is that acting on a per-category audit has its own finite-sample price, because each category that gets its own rule needs enough data before that rule can be trusted. The public result closest to this is the conformal paper above, where the guarantee attaches to a single act-or-escalate rule calibrated on held-out questions, and the price is paid in escalation: at α = 0.05 the system acts on only 6.2% of MMLU-Pro Law questions and escalates the rest.

Looking back, this was less a change of direction than a return, in sharper form, to the questions of risk management: not only whether an estimate is accurate, but which decision it licenses, under which assumptions and at what tolerance. Capacity, audit and decision is that sequence applied to a model's judgment of itself.

Put simply, I stopped asking "is this model calibrated?" and started asking "what decision does this score support, and at what cost?" The other projects had been pointing that way already. The conformal guarantee is attached to a decision rule, act or escalate, not to the agents' confidence. In the clarification study, a rating-only ablation that scored actions without ever acting on the score landed 0.9% below the baseline; in that comparison the gain appeared only when the score was allowed to decide. And in R3 the gain depends on how feedback is turned into training data, as the structured-reflection ablation shows. For self-improvement, the same question has a specific form: the decision is whether to learn from the model's own selection, and the cost is whatever errors that selection carries into training.

Key Insight

A self-assessment earns its value through the decision it supports. Calibration is a property of a score; reliability is a property of what a system does with that score, and the second needs its own evidence.

The Mathematics Underneath

I have not left the mathematics behind, and I do not think of it as a separate interest. This year I posted Rényi Stability of Bh Sets, a single-author preprint in combinatorics and information theory. It asks how much probability mass must be removed so that what remains is collision-free, given that the sum map loses only a little Rényi entropy, and it gives a sharp phase diagram in two Rényi orders. It says nothing about language models. What carries over is a habit: state exactly which quantity controls which, and what the bound costs. The move from capacity to audit to decision is that habit applied to my own earlier results.

What Is Still Open

Several questions remain open, and I would rather list them than claim they are settled.

Calibrated deferral mid-task. The conformal work gives a guarantee at the end of a decision; the clarification work asks mid-trajectory using uncalibrated ordinal scores. Bringing calibrated guarantees to intermediate decisions is something we have not yet done. Who answers. Asking well is only useful if the answer is good. We have compared a controlled answer source with a degraded one, but how the value of asking changes across the range in between, from a knowledgeable person to retrieval or a weaker model, we have not measured. Improvement without a strong teacher. R3 depends on a teacher. Whether a model's own evaluation, once audited, can stand in for part of that feedback is the question that would turn self-evaluation into self-improvement, and our results do not yet answer it. The follow-up manuscript shows how much is missing: across 17,420 self-evaluations from 13 models and four benchmarks, reported confidence was on average only 0.005 higher when the model's choice agreed with the reference than when it did not, and 21 of the 52 model and dataset pairs had the gap the wrong way round. References that are not ground truth. If judges are references, then audits inherit their biases, and we need better ways to report that uncertainty alongside the audit.

The starting intuition still holds my attention: a model that can choose between its own candidates might be able to learn from that choice. The goal has stayed the same since the active learning days, a system that knows where its judgment is weak, asks for help there, and gets better from what it hears back. What has changed is how carefully I separate those three steps, how much evidence I ask of each one before calling a system reliable, and the question I now put to any score before letting it select, defer or train: what decision does this score support, and at what cost?
